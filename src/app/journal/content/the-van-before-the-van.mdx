This entry starts with a life transition I am not going to write about on a
public page. The part that belongs here is the part that is engineering: I need
a place to live and a place to work, they need to be the same place, and that
place needs wheels. A Ford Transit converted into a studio apartment and a
development studio, driven through the gap between one home and the next.

On 2026-08-12 I gave that idea one working day and no hardware budget. The rule
for the day was the same rule I use on every build: nothing gets bought until
the thing it is being bought for has a number attached to it.

## The spec had to survive a grilling

The first hour was not a search. It was an argument, on purpose, against my own
preferences. Length, series, drivetrain, engine, roof, seating: each one got
pushed until it either produced a reason or fell over.

What came out the other side is narrow enough to be useful as a filter. Ford
Transit, T-350 series for the payload headroom, factory all wheel drive, high
roof, 148 inch regular wheelbase rather than extended so it still parks on a
street, and the turbocharged 3.5L EcoBoost rather than the naturally aspirated
3.5L that most of these vans are built with. A certified second row for my boys
is a purchase specification, not a build item, because seat anchorages are one
of the few things in a van you cannot add later in good conscience.

The wheelbase argument is the one I expect to reopen. The build program presses
against 148 inches and street parking presses back, and I would rather have that
fight in a model than in a parking spot.

## A ledger before a shopping list

The second decision of the day was that this project runs on ledgers rather than
on a shopping list. Every part carries a weight, a price and a watt-hour cost,
and it enters the record at the moment it is chosen, not after it is installed.
A van conversion is a payload budget wearing a floor plan.

The electrical design is where that discipline paid first. The load came before
the battery: a parked working day burns about 4.8 kWh, the development rig and
the satellite link being about half of it, and winter turns out to land within 25 Wh
of summer because the heater burns gasoline rather than amps. Craven's Caravans
priced 3 complete electrical tiers at $4,893, $8,346 and $13,892 before buying a
single component, and the middle one wins: 920Ah of Victron, about 350 pounds,
2 full winter working days of autonomy.

The number I liked least is the one that changed the plan. Weekly use is roughly
33 kWh against about 12 kWh of realistic summer recovery from driving and solar,
which means a parked van runs a daily deficit no battery chemistry argues its way
out of. So winter ski trips are shore power trips, and the roof mounted air
conditioner gets deferred behind one summer of real telemetry instead of being
bought on a feeling. The circuit for it gets run anyway while the walls are open.

## Five phases, and a van that always drives

The build plan is staggered by necessity rather than by tidiness. Sound
deadening, every roof penetration in a single sealing campaign, insulation,
floor, then the electrical spine, then water, then the living machine. Two
laws hold it together: everything that is easier in an empty van happens before
anything covers a wall, and every phase ends with a van that still drives and
still camps. Weigh empty, weigh after the electrical, weigh at the end.

## A room with three people in it

My sister is in this project properly, which meant she needed the same view of
it that I have rather than a summary of it after the fact. That turned into 192
lines of Node with no dependencies: a long poller against the Telegram API, a
sender behind an operations gate, and a launchd service so it survives a reboot
without me thinking about it. Messages land in a JSONL inbox that gets read at
the start of every session.

It went live at 16:34 that afternoon. The first message in the group was two
words: "The beginning."

## The argument needed a model

The wheelbase question, the roof real estate question, the where does the wet
bath go question: all three are spatial arguments, and spatial arguments held in
prose go in circles. So the last thing built that day was the thing that lets
the arguments end.

It is a parametric Transit shell in React Three Fiber, both wheelbase variants,
with the build program expressed as 8 typed modules you drag onto a 1 inch grid.
Every module carries its real weight, its real cost and its real average draw,
and three ledgers total live: weight against the series payload, cost against the
build cap, watts as a running load. Footprint collisions light up amber. An
exploded view pulls the whole interior apart. The layout persists, so a session
survives closing the tab.

It is a private tool for one van right now, and it is deliberately a drawing
board rather than a product. Whether it becomes one depends on whether it
survives being used to build this van, which is the only evidence that would
count.

## One van in the country

The specification is narrow, and narrow specifications are worth checking against
supply before falling in love with them. The national pool of Transit 350 all
wheel drive high roofs on this wheelbase holds roughly 16 to 20 units at any
given moment, priced from $29,000 to $58,000, and buyers outnumber vans.

The engine is the filter that does the work. Most all wheel drive Transits carry
the naturally aspirated engine, and position 8 of the VIN tells you which one you
are looking at in about ten seconds, which is a great deal cheaper than finding
out at the dealership. Craven's Caravans searched the whole country and came back
with 1 retail van whose engine is confirmed by its VIN, a 2023 T-350 with 31,988
miles at $42,995 sitting outside Detroit. There is a second one, an ex-municipal auction van carrying a replacement
engine with about 8,000 miles on it, closing tomorrow morning.

## How the day was actually run

Most of that day I spent deciding rather than typing. The grilling, the ledger
discipline, the ruling that the national search was worth a flight, the call to
defer the air conditioner, the choice to build the configurator before buying
the van: those are the day's actual content, and none of them are automatable
because each one is a trade I have to live inside.

What made the volume possible is that the research, the sweeps and the
scaffolding ran in parallel while I worked the decisions. Agents are extremely
good at going and finding out, and extremely bad at knowing which answer you can
afford. The bottleneck in a day like this is judgment, and the way you get more
out of the day is to stop spending judgment on retrieval.

The van is not bought. The auction closes in the morning, the wire has not
landed, and the wheelbase argument is still open. But there is a specification
with reasons behind it, a power design with a bill of materials, a build plan
with a calendar, a room where three of us can talk, and a model to argue in. The
van is the last thing that shows up, and that is the correct order.
